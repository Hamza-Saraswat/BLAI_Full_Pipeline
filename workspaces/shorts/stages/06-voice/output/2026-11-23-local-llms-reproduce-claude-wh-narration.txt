You pay for Claude every month. Then a post says you could run it at home for free. One developer actually tried it. His box is a home P C, two generations old, with a secondhand graphics card. It runs local models, A I stored as files on his own machine. His test was one hard job he built himself: rebuild a research simulation as working code. On that job, the local models landed very close to Claude Sonnet. So what did he measure? Claude's app usually streams sixty to eighty tokens a second, tokens being the little chunks of words it writes. His big local models? Thirty tokens a second. He says he's happy to get it. That's just under half the speed of a subscription. So how does a giant model fit on a gaming card? Quantization, storing the model's numbers with fewer bits, shrinks the file until it fits in your card's memory. The other trick is mixture of experts, waking only a few specialist parts per token. A huge model ends up running at small-model speed. Now the verdict he wrote. His best local models edge past last year's Claude and almost tie today's Sonnet. The keyword, he writes, is almost. No blind test, no head-to-head benchmark, and no drop-in replacement for the top Claude. In another engineer's test, a leaderboard favorite did nothing while a plainer model finished. Think of a local model as a cover band that has rehearsed your setlist. On the songs it knows, it sounds like the record. His simulation job was one of those songs. Shout a request it never learned, and it fumbles. So Claude at home is real, and it's a trade, not a replacement. Even the man who ran it still pays ten dollars a month for the original.
